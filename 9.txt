অষ্টমী — The Ones We Come Home To

Ashtami with Ma and Baba.

The usual getting-ready chaos,
someone taking too long,
someone complaining about the traffic,
and the inevitable “ar koto khon lagbe?”
before we’ve even stepped out of the house.

There were pandals to visit,
photos to take,
and the familiar arguments
that somehow manage to find their way
into every family outing.

Ektu edik-odik niye jhogra,
ektu rag, ektu obhiman.
Someone would get irritated,
someone would go quiet,
and a little while later,
we’d be talking about something else entirely.

Nothing unusual.
Just us being us.

We walked through the crowds together,
stopped wherever the queue allowed,
and tried to make the most of the evening.
Ma would notice something I’d walked past.
Baba would have his own opinion about the arrangements.
I’d probably be busy checking my phone
or asking how much farther we had to walk.

Ordinary things.
Things I don’t usually think twice about.

Except this time,
there was a thought sitting somewhere in the background.

This might be the last Ashtami
I spend at home with them for a while.

Next year, there could be work,
different responsibilities,
a city that isn’t Kolkata,
or simply a life that doesn’t let me come home
as easily as it does now.

Nothing is certain yet.
But the possibility was enough
to make the evening feel a little different.

I looked at Ma and Baba
and caught myself noticing things
I would usually take for granted—
the way they waited for me,
the small instructions I’d heard a hundred times,
the familiar complaints,
even the arguments I sometimes wished would stop.

Because one day,
I might find myself wishing
for one more of these very ordinary evenings.

One more “kheyechish?”
One more reminder to be careful in the crowd.
One more disagreement over something insignificant,
followed by the comfort of knowing
we’d all be sitting together at home later.

And I don’t think they realise
how much of this I’m quietly holding on to.

Or maybe they do,
in their own way.

We don’t always know how to be sentimental
with the people we’re closest to.
Sometimes affection comes disguised as irritation,
concern sounds like a lecture,
and love is simply someone making sure
you’ve eaten before leaving the house.

That evening, I didn’t say any of this.
We were too busy being a family,
with all the jhogra, rag-ragi and familiar nonsense
that comes with it.

We took our pictures.
We walked through the pandals.
We came home tired,
probably with a few things left unsaid.

And maybe that’s what made it feel real.

I don’t want to turn every ordinary moment
into a goodbye before it has even ended.
I don’t know where I’ll be next year,
or whether this really will be our last Pujo together
for a while.

I only know that growing up
sometimes means realising
that the life you’ve been impatient to leave
is also the one you’ll miss the most.

Ashtami was just another Pujo evening
with Ma and Baba.

And somewhere between the arguments
and the photographs,
I found myself wishing
we could have a few more years
of evenings exactly like this one.

Not perfect.
Not without the usual fuss.

Just us,
together,
at home for Pujo.
