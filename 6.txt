ষষ্ঠী — Ei Gang Ta Alada Chhilo

Sasthi with college friends.
The mandatory annual outing,
the one plan nobody really needs to make
because “North Kolkata jabi?”
is usually enough.

And off we go.

Someone’s late, obviously.
Someone hasn’t even left home.
Someone’s calling every two minutes to ask,
“Kothay achish tora?”
while being the furthest one from the meeting point.

Classic.

North Kolkata was as beautiful as ever.
Old houses, narrow lanes,
the lights, the pandals,
and crowds moving in every possible direction.
We squeezed through the bhir,
argued over which pujo to see next,
and stopped every few minutes
because somebody wanted a photo.

Then came the usual business.

Leg-pulling that should probably be illegal.
Old stories being brought up at the worst possible time.
One friend getting roasted for something
that happened two years ago.
And the whole group laughing like
we’d collectively lost our minds.

And, of course, the meyeder dekhe scene.

The sudden elbow nudges.
The unnecessary commentary.
The friend who’d been talking absolute nonsense
two seconds ago suddenly fixing his hair
and pretending he hadn’t noticed anyone.

“Dhur, bhai, chup kor!”

Which, naturally, made everyone louder.

We had phuchka, probably discussed food again
before we’d even finished eating,
and kept walking without any real destination.
Every lane had something to stop for;
every stop somehow turned into another adda.

Nothing extraordinary happened that day.
That’s probably why I liked it so much.

Just my college people,
the kind of nonsense you can’t explain at home,
inside jokes that make no sense to outsiders,
and that rare comfort of being around people
who’ve seen enough of you
to never let you live anything down.

Except, somewhere between the laughter
and the endless “chol, oi dike jai”,
a small thought crossed my mind.

This could be our last one like this.

Next year, who knows?
Placements, jobs, higher studies,
different cities, different schedules.
We’ll still have the group chat, obviously.
We’ll send reels, abuse each other in the comments,
and make plans that take three months to happen.

But will we all be here?
Walking through North Kolkata on Sasthi,
with nowhere urgent to be
and the whole evening still ahead of us?

I didn’t say any of this.
What would I even say?

Instead, I joined the next argument,
laughed at the next stupid joke,
and let someone bring up that embarrassing story
one more bloody time.

Because that’s what you do, I guess.
You don’t make a farewell speech
in the middle of a Pujo outing.
You just stay a little longer.

Take one more group picture.
Wait for the friend who’s fallen behind.
Say “last pandal, pakka”
and then somehow end up at another one.

Sasthi was full of the usual madness.
Maybe that’s what I’ll miss the most—
not any particular pandal or photograph,
but having all of us together,
being absolutely insufferable,
and not realising how special
an ordinary day with your people can be.
