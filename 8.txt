সপ্তমী, দ্বিতীয় দিন — The Morning After

The next morning, I woke up with a slight hangover.

Not just from the cigarettes, the rain,
or the long afternoon that somehow became evening.
There was something else lingering, too—
something I couldn’t quite sleep off.

Yesterday kept coming back in pieces.

The shade we sat under when it started raining.
The way we ran through the streets,
already drenched and not particularly bothered.
That little gully, the conversations,
the number exchanged before we went our separate ways.

Nothing extraordinary, if you told the story to someone else.

But then, why was I thinking about it so much?

I went about my morning as usual.
Checked my phone, put it down,
picked it up again a little later.
Scrolled through the same things
without really taking any of them in.

And every now and then,
my mind would find its way back to her.

Kalke ki shotti kichhu chhilo,
na ami nijer moto kore bhebe nichhi?

I hated that I couldn’t tell.

Maybe she had enjoyed the afternoon, too.
Maybe she’d gone home, slept well,
and woken up thinking about absolutely nothing.
Maybe the rain, the conversations, the little moments
were just another Pujo memory for her.

And that would be perfectly fine.

Except, somewhere between knowing that
and actually being okay with it,
there was this strange, uncomfortable space.

I’d only known her for a few hours.
What right did I have to miss someone
I hadn’t even properly met?

Still, there I was,
trying to remember the exact things she’d said,
wondering if I’d imagined the ease between us,
replaying conversations that probably meant
nothing more than what they were.

Baire tokhon Pujor shohor.

The para was waking up,
people were getting ready,
someone downstairs was talking loudly on the phone,
and somewhere in the distance,
a dhaak was practising for the evening.

Kolkata had no intention of slowing down
just because I couldn’t get my head straight.

The city gives you a thousand reasons to go out,
a hundred places to be,
so many people to meet.

Yet sometimes, one person you met by chance
can occupy more of your mind
than everything else put together.

I wondered whether I should text her.

What would I even say?

Kal bhalo laglo?
Too obvious, perhaps.
A random reel? A casual message?
Or should I let the conversation happen naturally,
the way yesterday had?

I kept going back and forth,
making a whole situation out of a message
I hadn’t even sent.

The worst part wasn’t the uncertainty itself.
It was how quickly I’d started caring about the answer.

I wasn’t in love.
I barely knew her.

But there was a spark, or at least
something that felt enough like one
to make me curious about what came next.

And I think that frightened me a little.

Because it had been a while since I’d felt
that particular kind of excitement—
the kind that comes with no guarantees,
when you have nothing to hold on to
except a few memories and the possibility
that the other person might want to know you, too.

Perhaps this was the October theory
doing its work on me.

Perhaps I was just another person in Kolkata
getting carried away during Pujo,
mistaking a beautiful afternoon
for the beginning of a beautiful story.

I couldn’t tell the difference yet.

So I let the morning pass.
The phone stayed close.
The thoughts came and went.
And somewhere underneath the overthinking
was a small, stubborn hope
that yesterday hadn’t ended
when we said goodbye.

Maybe she was thinking about it, too.
Maybe she wasn’t.

But for the first time that Pujo,
I found myself looking forward to the next day
for a reason I couldn’t tell my friends about
without being thoroughly bullied.

“Ki re, byapar ki?”

I could already hear them.

And honestly, they’d probably have a point.

Still, I hoped I’d see her again.

Not because yesterday had promised me anything,
but because it had left me with a question
I genuinely wanted to know the answer to.

Pujo cholchhilo nijer chhonde.
Ar ami, eto manusher bhirer moddhe,
chupchap ekjoner abar dekha pawar opekkhay chhilam.
