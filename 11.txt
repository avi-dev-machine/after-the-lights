দশমী — Until We Meet Again

Dashami has a way of making everything feel a little quieter.

The same city that spent the last few days refusing to sleep
now seems to be preparing itself for the goodbye.
The dhaak sounds a little different,
the smiles carry a little sadness,
and somewhere between the sindoor and the visarjan,
you realise it’s time to let go again.

I’m sitting at my desk,
trying to put this Pujo into words.

Funny, isn’t it?
Spending the last few days living through moments
and spending Dashami trying to understand what they meant.

I’ve been writing about feelings
I couldn’t always explain while they were happening.
Little moments, unexpected connections,
the warmth of familiar faces,
the strange ache of knowing that some things might change.

Maybe that’s what I wanted these poems to do—
hold on to the things that would otherwise slip away.

Because photographs are good at remembering
what a moment looked like.
But they don’t always remember
what it felt like to be there.

And this year, there was so much to feel.

The happiness of being around your people.
The comfort of home.
The excitement of something new.
The loneliness that visits without an invitation.
The quiet fear of growing up
and finding that the things you took for granted
are slowly becoming things you have to make time for.

I don’t think I understood all of it yet.
Perhaps I still don’t.

But somewhere between living these days
and sitting down to write about them,
I realised that Pujo had become more than a celebration.

It had become a collection of little reminders
of the life I’m living right now—
the people in it, the things I’m feeling,
the things I’m still hoping for,
and all the things I haven’t quite learnt to say aloud.

And maybe that’s why Dashami feels different.

It’s not just that the idols will leave,
or that the lights will come down
and the city will slowly return to its usual rhythm.

It’s that life will move on, too.

College will get busy.
People will get caught up in their own lives.
Some conversations will continue,
some might fade,
and some new stories might still be waiting to begin.

We don’t know yet.

For now, there’s Bhashan,
the last few photographs,
the familiar “ashche bochhor abar hobe”,
and that strange feeling of being happy
that something happened
while already wishing it hadn’t ended.

I suppose growing up is learning
that you can be grateful for a moment
without knowing whether you’ll ever get it back.

So I’ll finish writing this poem,
close the notebook,
and let Dashami be Dashami.

There will be other Octobers,
other Pujos, other people and places.
Some things will change; others might surprise me.

And when the time comes,
I’ll probably find myself back at this desk again,
trying to make sense of it all in words.

Maybe that’s my way of saying goodbye—
not by holding on to Pujo itself,
but by writing down what it left behind in me.

Bhalo theko, Pujo.

Ashche bochhor abar hobe.
